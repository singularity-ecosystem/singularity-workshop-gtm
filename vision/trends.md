# Trends in AI adoption and employee displacement

## Abstract

Generative AI is exposing a large share of work tasks to automation. Institutional forecasts put the automatable share near a quarter to 30% of hours in the U.S. and Europe by 2030, while the World Economic Forum expects more jobs created than displaced over the same period. Payroll records since late 2022 show employment falling for the youngest workers in automatable occupations and holding or rising for older workers in those same jobs. Employers are paying a large premium for AI skills, and some now treat effective AI use as a condition of headcount and performance.

## Task exposure

Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, and Rock at OpenAI and the University of Pennsylvania ([GPTs are GPTs](https://openai.com/index/gpts-are-gpts/), 2023):

- About **80% of U.S. workers** are in occupations where large language models could affect **at least 10% of tasks**.
- About **19% of workers** are in occupations where **at least half of tasks** are exposed, once software built on those models is included.
- With an LLM alone, about **15% of all U.S. work tasks** could be done much faster at the same quality. With tools built on top of LLMs, that rises to **47–56% of tasks**.
- Exposure spans wage levels. The authors find it rare for a model to be able to do nearly all of an occupation’s work. Their measure is technical capacity to speed up tasks, which depends on adoption, regulation, and firm choices before it becomes job loss.

Across the institutional reports, a large share of tasks comes off the human workload, a smaller share of jobs is displaced, and new jobs are projected alongside the losses. The CEO forecasts are sharper and concentrate the loss on white-collar work and on the entry level.

## Employment, wages, and hiring

**Early-career employment in automatable jobs is down. Older workers in the same jobs are not.**

Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen, [Canaries in the Coal Mine](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/) (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, November 2025), use ADP payroll data:

- From late 2022 to September 2025, employment of workers **ages 22–25** in the most AI-exposed occupations **fell about 6%**. Employment of **older workers in those same occupations rose 6–9%**.
- After controlling for firm-level shocks, workers ages 22–25 in the most exposed quintile of jobs had about a **15 log-point** relative employment decline versus the least exposed quintile. Estimates for older age groups are small.
- The decline shows up where AI **automates** the work (software development, customer service). Occupations where AI use is mostly **augmentative** still added young workers.
- Overall employment kept growing. The adjustment in this data is fewer new seats at the bottom of automatable occupations.

A U.S. Census Bureau working paper, [Graduating Into Disruption](https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/ces/CES-WP-26-56.pdf) (CES-WP-26-56), finds the same cut at the point of hiring. In the most AI-exposed decile of college majors, largely computer science, information systems, and other software-related fields, the chance of having a job soon after graduation fell by **5 percentage points** after ChatGPT, and starting earnings fell **13%**. About half of that earnings drop is graduates landing in lower-paid sectors such as retail and food service. Effects fade with time in the market and remain large for the most exposed majors.

**Employers are hiring and paying for AI skill.**

- World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs 2025: **two-thirds of employers** plan to hire people with specific AI skills. **AI and big data** is the fastest-growing skill they named, ahead of networks, cybersecurity, and general technology literacy. **85%** plan to prioritize upskilling the people they already have. Skill gaps are the barrier named by **63%** of employers.
- [PwC 2025 Global AI Jobs Barometer](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/artificial-intelligence/job-barometer/2025/executive-summary.pdf), from nearly a billion job ads: workers with AI skills (machine learning, prompt engineering, and similar) earn a **56% wage premium** over people in the same occupation without those skills, up from **25%** the year before. The premium shows up in every industry they analyzed. Skills demanded by employers are changing **66% faster** in the occupations most exposed to AI. Job postings that ask for AI skills grew **7.5%** while total postings fell **11.3%**. PwC also finds jobs and wages still growing in highly automatable occupations. The premium attaches to people who can use the tools, including inside roles that are being automated.
- Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke, internal memo, March 2025 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/04/07/shopify-ceo-prove-ai-cant-do-jobs-before-asking-for-more-headcount.html)): “Using AI effectively is now a fundamental expectation of everyone at Shopify.” Teams must show why AI cannot do the work before they ask for more headcount. AI use goes into performance and peer reviews. He told staff that opting out of learning to apply AI leads to stagnation. Shopify’s headcount fell from about 11,600 in 2022 to 8,100 at the end of 2024 while revenue kept growing.

**On the tasks AI can do, the measured productivity gain is largest for less experienced workers.**

In Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond’s customer-support study (NBER, 2023), a conversational assistant raised productivity about **14%** on average and about **34%** for novice and lower-skilled agents, with little gain for the most experienced agents. Experience still shows up in the payroll results above: older workers in exposed occupations have kept employment while entry-level hiring in automatable work has shrunk. The scarce work left in those jobs is judgment, clients, and exceptions. Domain experts who can direct the tools are the group still growing inside exposed occupations. New graduates in the most exposed majors are the group losing the first job.

## Summary

Studies converge on partial automation of a large share of tasks, with job loss so far concentrated in early-career hiring for work AI can do end to end. Goldman Sachs, McKinsey, and the World Economic Forum describe roughly a quarter to 30% of hours at stake and a labor market that still adds jobs in aggregate. Amodei and Farley describe a much faster loss of white-collar and entry-level roles. Since late 2022, workers ages 22–25 in the most exposed jobs and graduates of the most exposed majors have seen employment and starting pay fall, while older workers in the same occupations have not. Firms are staffing for that gap: two-thirds of employers in the World Economic Forum survey plan to hire AI skills, PwC finds a 56% wage premium for those skills, and Shopify has made AI use a requirement for performance and for new headcount.
